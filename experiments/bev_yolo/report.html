Capstone · vision-based landing · experiment iteration 3

The Centroid Swap

Ryu et al. read the runway’s cross-track offset from two points. Reading the whole mask instead cut mean error 35% and the worst frame by 4× — and fixed an error curve that had been pointing the wrong way.

Dataset X-Plane 12 · 118 episodes · 50,820 frames Split test, n=567, airport-grouped Detector YOLOv8s-seg on BEV Heading from INS
01

What changed

The estimator is unchanged from Ryu et al. (IJRR 44(6), 2024) in every respect but one: which point it reads off the segmentation mask. The paper takes the midpoint of the runway’s front edge — two points. This iteration takes the polygon’s area centroid instead. Both lie on the runway centre line, so the downstream geometry is identical; only the robustness differs.

For reference, the paper reports 1.779 m MAE / 2.380 m RMSE over eight real flight tests. This pipeline now sits below that on synthetic data, without the Kalman filter doing any of the work.

02

The symptom: error pointed the wrong way

Cross-track error from a distant object is range · sin δψ — so it should shrink as the aircraft closes in. It did the opposite. The two-point estimator was worst in the nearest range bin and best in the farthest, which is a signal that something other than geometry is dominating.

03

The diagnosis: a tail, not a bias

It is not mask quality. The predicted mask’s area is within 0.3% of the label’s at every range, and its fitted width is good to 0.64 m inside 300 m. The median error of the front-edge midpoint is flat with range too, at −0.9 to −1.4 m.

What grows is the tail. The worst frames put the threshold midpoint 13–23 m off — about half a runway width (45 m ÷ 2 = 22.5 m). That is the signature of the two-point rule latching onto a partial front edge: read two points, and a clipped edge or a rounded corner slides their midpoint by up to half a width.

04

Choosing the shape

If two points are fragile, fit something to the whole mask. Three candidates were considered; the BEV canvas keeps square metric pixels by construction, which decides the question.

◇

Rhombus

Rejected

A rhombus has four equal sides. A 45 × 2500 m runway is not remotely that. A general parallelogram would only be correct under anisotropic BEV scaling, which this pipeline deliberately does not have.

▭

Smallest enclosing rectangle

Partial

The right shape — in a metric BEV a runway is exactly a rectangle — but the wrong estimator. It is a maximum statistic, set by the outermost pixels, so it fixes the near field (2.50 → 2.08 m) and degrades at range (1.69 → 2.34 m), where the mask’s far end is smeared.

✛

Polygon area centroid

Adopted

Also on the centre line, but an average over the whole shape rather than an extremum. Cross-track needs any point on the centre line — not the threshold specifically — and far-end clipping, which every frame here has, is laterally symmetric and so cannot bias it.

Why the area centroid, not the vertex mean

Douglas–Peucker leaves vertices unevenly spaced, so a vertex mean double-counts corners that happen to carry two nearby points. In the unit test, two extra collinear vertices move the area centroid 0.01 m and the vertex mean 104 m.

06

What limits what

The oracle — the ground-truth label pushed through the same estimator — is the floor no detector can beat. Reading it alongside the predicted result says which half of the pipeline is the bottleneck, and the answer inverts between the two variants.

The centroid trades accuracy on a perfect mask for robustness on a real one, and that trade pays because real masks are what the system gets. But its worse floor is worth explaining, because it is not a property of the method.

The headroom this exposes

On correct geometry the centroid estimator is exact to 0.007 m in synthetic test. Fixing the label rotation would move paper_axis from 1.22 m toward 0.3 m — more headroom than any other change currently on the roadmap.

07

Three caveats to carry

1 · Heading comes from the INS

Every number above uses the true heading, as Ryu et al. do — they have an INS and never claim to estimate heading from the camera. The camera-only figure is fitted_yaw at 6.21 m MAE. Do not quote 1.22 m against a camera-only baseline without saying so.

2 · The Kalman filter is now nearly redundant

It moves MAE 1.224 → 1.224 m. Most of what it was buying on the two-point estimator was absorbing exactly the outliers the shape fit now prevents. It still trims the worst frame (4.77 → 3.52 m), which is where it earns its place.

3 · This weakens the earlier “BEV beats perspective” claim

The same swap takes the perspective arm from 4.833 → 1.100 m MAE, putting it back ahead of BEV’s 1.224 m. That earlier result was substantially about which estimator tolerates a heavy-tailed mask, not about the view. BEV keeps a far better worst case (4.8 m vs 36.1 m), so the honest summary is now “BEV is more robust, perspective is more accurate.”

One scope note: the centroid estimates cross-track only. Its along-track output is the centroid’s position, not the threshold’s, so the range estimate the paper also wants is given up. For a system judged on lateral guidance that is the right trade, but it is a trade.
